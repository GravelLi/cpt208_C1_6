Motivation & Research

Why this direction matters and where design opportunity appears

This page frames the value of HerRun through motivation, comparison, and stakeholder understanding. It establishes why the project matters in the first place before later pages move into requirements, ideas, and implementation.

The Why

Designing a Women-Centred Running System for Wellbeing and Support

In today's fast-paced daily life, many people want to use running as a simple way to build their body. However, for many female runners, running is not only about pace, distance, or competition. It is also closely related to emotional wellbeing, body condition, safety, comfort, and whether the experience feels sustainable in everyday life. Existing running apps often focus heavily on performance tracking, rankings, and training efficiency. While these functions may suit performance-oriented runners, they do not fully respond to users who seek a low-pressure, supportive, and more human-centred running experience.

Our group therefore chose the user-centric supportive running companion because we wanted to explore how digital design could encourage healthier and more sustainable exercise habits in a way that reflects real users' lived experiences. Through early research, we found that many potential users, especially female runners, need more than a basic running tracker. They need a system that connects mood awareness, flexible running styles, practical health support, and a sense of safety and companionship. Based on this, we propose HerRun, a women-centred running web application designed to make running feel lighter, safer, and more emotionally supportive, helping users build a healthier and more sustainable relationship with exercise.

The Gap

Comparison across academic papers and commercial products

These placeholders show the structure we will use to compare four academic references and four commercial products, focusing on what each one supports well and what it still misses.

Academic Papers

Four structured placeholders for literature comparison.

Paper 1

Did well
  • Explained motivation patterns among student runners.
  • Connected activity with belonging and peer support.
  • Highlighted barriers to sustained participation.
Missed
  • Did not address real-time coordination.
  • Offered little safety-focused guidance.
  • Lacked product-level implications.

Paper 2

Did well
  • Discussed community-based motivation.
  • Compared short- and long-term engagement.
  • Suggested habit-forming support.
Missed
  • Focused more on theory than interface design.
  • Did not include organizer perspectives.
  • Ignored onboarding anxiety.

Paper 3

Did well
  • Examined trust in group activities.
  • Raised questions about communication clarity.
  • Suggested ways to reduce uncertainty.
Missed
  • Did not focus on running contexts.
  • Overlooked emotional safety for women runners.
  • Provided limited digital comparison.

Paper 4

Did well
  • Investigated wellbeing outcomes.
  • Supported the value of encouragement.
  • Made a case for accessible activity spaces.
Missed
  • Did not explore scheduling friction.
  • Left organizer workflows underdeveloped.
  • Did not map event discovery clearly.
Commercial Products

Four placeholders for product comparison.

Product 1

Did well
  • Offered clear event discovery.
  • Presented progress visually.
  • Made participation more visible.
Missed
  • Weak support for group safety expectations.
  • Limited campus specificity.
  • Did not help organizers communicate well.

Product 2

Did well
  • Included strong social cues.
  • Encouraged routine through reminders.
  • Reduced friction in joining activities.
Missed
  • Did not distinguish new and experienced runners.
  • Safety information was weak.
  • Administration tools were minimal.

Product 3

Did well
  • Supported route planning and coordination.
  • Made logistics easier to understand.
  • Provided utility for repeated activities.
Missed
  • Felt task-heavy rather than welcoming.
  • Social belonging was under-emphasized.
  • Motivation cues lacked warmth.

Product 4

Did well
  • Created trust with clear status patterns.
  • Handled reminders effectively.
  • Reduced confusion with structured updates.
Missed
  • Did not address hesitation before joining.
  • Offered weak support for inclusive tone.
  • Lacked a university-specific experience.
